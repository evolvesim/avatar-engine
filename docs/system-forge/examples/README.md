# Worked examples

Three systems expressed in the [`GameSystem` schema](../03-schema.md). They exist to answer one
question: **does the model actually generalise, or does it only fit the family it was designed
around?**

Each was chosen for a different degree of divergence from its lineage.

| File | Lineage | Divergence | What it proves |
|---|---|---|---|
| [`d20-fork.json`](./d20-fork.json) | d20 class-and-level | Modest | A familiar family survives two real mutations — a new outcome band and a flattened power curve — without the rest of the document fighting it |
| [`pbta-fork.json`](./pbta-fork.json) | 2d6 move-driven | Minimal | A system with no skill list, no initiative, no difficulty ladder and no GM rolls fills the schema without empty scaffolding |
| [`dicepool-fork.json`](./dicepool-fork.json) | Dice pool | Heavy | "Complex modifications" are reachable with typed primitives alone — no scripting |

## What each one is actually testing

### `d20-fork.json` — Ironbound

The mutation that matters is the **partial-success band**. Adding "bought at a price" to a
binary-outcome family is the most common homebrew move there is, and it's the one that most often
goes wrong: designers add the band and give it nothing to consume. Here it feeds a second harm
track (`strain`), which is what keeps the `band-consumed` validator quiet.

The second mutation is quieter and more interesting: `difficulty.scalesWithProgression` is set to
`false`, the ceiling drops from 20 to 10, and `horizontalRatio` rises from 0.4 to 0.7. Those three
changes are one decision — *get off the treadmill* — and the schema has to hold them together.
Change only one and `bounded-accuracy-drift` fires.

### `pbta-fork.json` — The Last Shift

This is what the guided tier produces: fork a lineage, answer about a dozen questions, accept
everything else. Included because a schema that only looks good when fully populated is a bad
schema.

Note what's legitimately near-empty: `derived` is `[]`, `subsystems` is `[]`, `customRules` is
`[]`, `adversaryTemplate` is `[]`, and the difficulty ladder has exactly one entry that exists
only so the validators have an anchor to measure against. None of that is a gap — it's a system
that has answered those axes with "none," which
[`01-commonalities.md`](../01-commonalities.md) argues is a design choice rather than an absence.

The `exhaustion` track is worth a look: a six-box track with a zero penalty per box and
`recovery.rate: "never"`. It's a countdown to the end of the character's night, built from the
generic harm-track shape rather than from a bespoke mechanism.

### `dicepool-fork.json` — Gilt and Ash

The stress test. Its centrepiece is a **variable success threshold**: dice normally succeed on 8+,
but every 3 points of Corruption lowers that by 1, to a floor of 6. Corruption makes you better at
everything and is otherwise ruinous.

That is not a parameter any of the six lineages exposes, and it is the kind of thing a user would
reach for scripting to express. Here it's a clamped formula tree reading a harm track
(`chassis.derived[id=success-threshold]`). Because it's a tree rather than a script, the
probability engine can still compute exact curves at each corruption level — which is the entire
argument for the structured-composition constraint in
[`05-modification-tiers.md`](../05-modification-tiers.md).

Three other things it exercises:

- **A two-sided meta-currency.** Every point of Leverage a player spends banks a point of Debt for
  the GM. The player resource and the pressure device are one mechanism seen from both sides.
- **Paired custom rules with opposite signs.** `the-bargain-answers` grants dice at high
  corruption; `gilded-tongue` penalises social rolls at higher corruption still. "Power at a
  price" is the most common shape a homebrew subsystem takes, and it needs to be reachable without
  scripting.
- **A secondary axis** (`effect`) derived from margin, paying into a currency.

## Caveat

These are hand-authored against the spec, not generated by an implementation — there is no code
yet. They're checked for JSON validity and reviewed against the schema by inspection. When the
schema becomes real, `preset-goldens.test.ts` should parse all three and assert zero error-level
diagnostics; any mismatch found at that point is a bug in one or the other, and the resolution
should be recorded rather than quietly patched.
